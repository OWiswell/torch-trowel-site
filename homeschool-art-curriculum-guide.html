The real buyer question

How do I teach real art at home if I am not an artist?

You need structure.

A good curriculum should tell you what to print, what skill is being practiced, what to notice, and when to stop.

Your student needs room to work.

The page should not be so crowded with instruction that the student has nowhere to draw or think.

You need confidence without performance.

You should not have to draw a perfect example before the lesson can begin.

Friction, follow-through, and fit

The best curriculum is the one you can actually use consistently.

Setup friction

If every lesson requires supply hunting, video setup, or parent improvising, the curriculum will be harder to repeat.

Attention friction

Video can help demonstration, but it also puts another screen in the school day. Printable lessons keep attention on the page.

Parent confidence

If you feel like you must perform the art lesson perfectly, you may avoid starting. Support pages should lower that pressure.

Skill continuity

Projects can be fun, but students also need control, observation, shape, form, and practice over time.
