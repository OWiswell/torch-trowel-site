Unit 03 / Building Simple Shapes

What to do when the drawing feels too big.

Building Simple Shapes teaches students to stop wrestling with the whole object at once. They learn to find the basic shapes underneath the subject and begin there.

Matt Tolbert July 7, 2026 4 min read

When a student says, “I can’t draw,” the drawing may simply feel too large to enter. The whole object is sitting there at once: edges, details, proportion, texture, and pressure.

Unit 03 gives the student a smaller doorway. Instead of trying to draw the finished thing immediately, they look for the simple shapes underneath it.

Shapes give the student somewhere to start.

A bottle may begin as a rectangle and two ellipses. A box may begin as a few angled planes. A complicated object may become a circle, a cylinder, or a group of simple forms before it becomes anything polished.

This does not make the drawing childish. It makes the drawing understandable. Construction lines let the student plan before they commit.

A complicated drawing becomes less intimidating when the student can name the first simple shape.

Construction lines reduce the pressure to be right.

Beginners often think every line has to be final. That makes the first mark feel risky. Construction lines change the mood of the page. They are allowed to search. They can be light, adjustable, and temporary.

That matters because students need permission to build a drawing. They do not have to produce it fully formed.

Parent note

Ask, “What is the largest simple shape you can find?” Then ask for one light construction line. Keep the first move small.

Why this follows line and observation.

By Unit 03, students have practiced controlling lines and seeing empty space. Now they begin using those skills to organize a subject. Simple shapes become a bridge between practice marks and real objects.

That bridge is important. It helps students move from “I can’t draw that” to “I can find the first shape.”

Lower the first step

Give your student one clear page to start.
