Older students

Middle school drawing lessons should respect the student and still teach foundations.

Older beginners often need basic drawing skills, but they do not want lessons that feel childish. The curriculum has to meet both needs.

Matt Tolbert July 7, 2026 4 min read

Middle school and high school students can be beginners without feeling young. That matters. A lesson can teach basic line, shape, and form without talking down to the student.

This is one reason many older homeschool students stall in art. Some resources are too childish. Others assume the student can already plan and execute an independent drawing. The useful middle ground is structured, skill-building practice with a serious tone.

Older beginners need foundations, not filler.

Line control, proportion, observation, shape, and form are not elementary skills in the dismissive sense. They are foundations. Professional artists keep returning to them.

The difference is presentation. A middle school student can practice controlled lines, but the lesson should explain why control matters. A high school student can draw simple forms, but the task should point toward objects, construction, and better seeing.

Do not make the parent carry the expertise.

Many parents want art in the homeschool week but do not want to become the art teacher. That is reasonable. The curriculum should carry the sequence and give the parent practical language for moments when the student gets stuck.

The parent does not need to critique every mark. They need to know the purpose of the exercise and the next question to ask.

What to look for

Choose lessons that are calm, clear, and age-respectful: not craft fluff, not open-ended overwhelm, and not a video that asks the student to copy passively.

The best lessons build confidence through evidence.

Confidence grows when the student can see improvement. A cleaner line, a better ellipse, a more believable form, or a clearer shape relationship gives the student proof that practice is working.

That is why Torch & Trowel begins with controlled drawing foundations. The early work is not busywork. It is the groundwork that lets older beginners become more independent.
