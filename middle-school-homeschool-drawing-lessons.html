Unit 02 / Seeing Empty Space

The lesson that teaches students to look before they draw.

Seeing Empty Space gives students a concrete way to observe. Instead of guessing what an object “should” look like, they learn to notice the shapes around it.

Matt Tolbert July 7, 2026 4 min read

After students begin to control their lines, the next challenge is seeing. Most beginners do not fail because they cannot move the pencil. They struggle because the subject feels too complicated to understand.

Seeing Empty Space gives them another way in. Instead of naming the object and drawing from memory, the student looks at the shapes around the object. That simple shift can make a complicated subject feel more manageable.

Negative space slows down guessing.

Students often draw what they think they know. A chair becomes “chair,” a cup becomes “cup,” and the hand starts using shortcuts before the eye has really looked.

Negative space interrupts that habit. The student asks, “What is the shape between these edges?” or “How wide is this opening compared to that one?” Those are answerable questions. They give the eye something specific to do.

Observation is a teachable skill.

Looking carefully is not the same as staring harder. Students need prompts that direct attention. Empty-space exercises give the student a structure for looking: compare spaces, follow edges, notice angles, and check relationships.

That is why this unit comes early. Before the Field Kit moves deeper into forms and objects, students need practice seeing what is there.

Parent note

When a student gets stuck, ask them to point to one empty shape around the object. Often the next line becomes clearer once they stop naming the object.

Seeing comes before correcting.

It is hard to fix a drawing if the student cannot yet see what is off. Seeing Empty Space builds that ability gently. The student learns to compare one shape to another, then use those comparisons to make better marks.

That is the reason Unit 02 belongs near the front of the sequence. It helps students move from “I know what this is” toward “I can see how this is shaped.”
